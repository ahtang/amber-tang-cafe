## 09. Disagree

**Triggers:** disagree, disagreement, conflict, pushback, ct o, cto, stakeholder disagreed, difficult feedback, engineer disagree, pm disagree, paywall argument, challenge a decision

I actually think disagreement is healthy—it can make the work better or more feasible. How I handle it depends on where we are in the process and how much data we have. On the HealthSherpa homepage, some people strongly wanted a notifications center because that's what users were asking for. I wasn't convinced it was the right approach, so while we were still exploring, we tested both concepts. The homepage performed better. In another project, a CTO disagreed with a paywall design, but we already had testing and an A/B experiment showing the version I'd proposed improved monetization by 3%, so we went with the design I originally recommended. When I don't have strong data, I might use support tickets, usability heuristics, or my own judgment, while recognizing implementation constraints. I don't think every disagreement needs to be 'won'—the point is to make the best call we can and keep learning.

At Calendly, we tested a getting-started guide that improved activation and some setup behaviors, like connecting a calendar or web conferencing, by around 2%. Afterward, our CEO felt the design was too task-based and wanted a more framework-based version within the next month. We didn't have time for new user interviews, so we talked to the CEO, reviewed engagement data, and kept the tasks that had shown positive results while changing the overall mental model. The next experiment left activation unchanged, which was a workable compromise between the user outcomes and the brand direction. I saw that as a calculated risk because we knew what evidence we had—and what we didn't.

## 35. Failed project

**Triggers:** failed project, project failed, failed experiment, failure example, pricing page experiments failed, three pricing experiments, pricing tabs, projected arr

In growth, failed experiments are pretty normal. One Calendly pricing-page project took three launches. We knew people struggled to compare plans. Our first attempt shortened the feature list, but paid conversion dropped. We learned that the higher-tier plans no longer looked clearly more valuable. The second version used tabbed comparison grids and performed flat versus the control. Research showed people missed the tabs, so we revised that. The third test still had flat paid conversion, but estimated projected ARR increased by about 2% because more people chose higher-tier plans. We learned something useful from each version instead of assuming the first one had to work.
